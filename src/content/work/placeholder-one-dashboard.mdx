## Context

What was this project, who was it for, and when did it happen? Two or three
sentences. Assume the reader has never heard of the client or the product.

## The problem

What was broken or missing before you started? Be concrete about the pain.
"The team exported CSVs and rebuilt the same chart by hand every Monday" tells a
reviewer far more than "the client needed a dashboard".

## My role

What did you personally own? If it was a team project, say who did what. Being
clear that you owned the front end while someone else did the API is a
credibility signal, not a weakness.

## Technical decisions

**This is the section reviewers actually read.** For each significant choice,
state the alternative you rejected and why.

- **Chose X over Y** because [constraint: bundle size, deadline, team
  familiarity, accessibility requirement].
- **Structured state as Z** because the data updated on two independent cycles
  and colocating it caused unnecessary re-renders.

## The hardest problem

Pick one genuinely difficult thing. Describe the symptom, what you tried, what
the actual cause turned out to be, and how you fixed it. Include the dead end.
Debugging narratives are the most convincing thing in a junior portfolio,
because they cannot be faked by a template.

## Outcome

What changed after it shipped? Measure it if you can: load time, task
completion time, users, error rate, conversion. If you have no numbers, describe
the qualitative change and say so honestly.

## What I would do differently

Show that you review your own work. Name a real limitation and the specific fix
you would apply with more time. This reads as maturity, not as weakness.
